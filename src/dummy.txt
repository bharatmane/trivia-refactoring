# Presentation objective

Create a 15–20-minute executive presentation explaining how enterprise applications may evolve beyond menus, navigation-heavy workflows and generic chatbots.

The central question is:

**Do users still need to navigate menus—or should AI have already analysed the work, prepared the insight, assembled the evidence and proposed the possible actions, waiting only for the user’s go/no-go decision?**

This is not a presentation about adding a chatbot to an existing application.

It is about redesigning the application around:

* What requires the user’s attention now.
* Why it requires attention.
* What evidence supports the conclusion.
* What action is recommended.
* What the system can execute after human authorization.
* How the action and its outcome remain auditable.

The central thesis is:

> **The future enterprise UI is not a chatbot. It is a prepared decision environment.**

The recurring control principle is:

> **AI prepares. Humans authorize. Systems execute. Everything is auditable.**

# Audience

The audience consists of Executive Directors, senior technology leaders, product leaders and business stakeholders in a large financial-services enterprise.

The presentation should be intellectually provocative, credible and grounded in a realistic proof of concept.

It must not feel like:

* A generic AI presentation.
* An AI history lesson.
* A product sales pitch.
* A collection of AI buzzwords.
* A chatbot demonstration.
* A detailed technical architecture review.
* A feature-by-feature product walkthrough.

The audience should leave reconsidering what the job of an enterprise user interface should be when the system already understands the user, role, operational context, policy, evidence and permitted actions.

# Proof-of-concept context

The proof of concept is a separate future-state enterprise application for collateral and margin operations. It is not a production capability.

All screenshots must carry or preserve this qualification:

**Future-state concept · Synthetic data · Illustrative experience · Not a production capability**

The application supports several personas:

* MORS — Margin Oversight and Risk users.
* Collateral Operations.
* Risk.
* Operations Senior.
* AI assistant, called IRIS.

The application is predominantly organised around:

**Call → Account → Evidence → Conversation → Decision → Action → Outcome**

Different personas use the same underlying account and margin-call data, but each persona has a different job, risk perspective and required action.

The future-state application should not merely give every persona a different menu. It should give each persona a different prepared decision surface.

# Business workflow

## Collateral Operations

When Collateral Operations users begin the day, they review operational exceptions.

Margin calls are generated. If an account has not been onboarded or does not have the required margin account or margin group, the user must resolve the onboarding problem before normal processing can continue.

Throughout the day, Collateral Operations may:

* Review call-generation exceptions.
* Verify that calls have been acknowledged.
* Communicate with internal teams and counterparties.
* Coordinate through email, Dynamics or Teams.
* Issue cash or security settlement instructions.
* Monitor whether instructions are accepted and settled.
* Investigate downstream source-system failures.
* Resolve failed IA updates.
* Track items approaching escalation or SLA thresholds.
* Intervene only where the workflow is not progressing normally.

The future-state experience should make healthy work quiet and bring only genuine interventions to the foreground.

## MORS

MORS users review generated calls and apply oversight controls.

Their activities include:

* Reviewing VM and IM calls above configured thresholds.
* Validating high-value calls.
* Investigating unusual calls even when they are below the normal threshold.
* Reviewing netting effects.
* Understanding the main products, trades and positions driving a call.
* Checking whether required commentary is present.
* Identifying new or existing trades where IA is required but has not been applied.
* Engaging Risk to determine or approve the appropriate IA.
* Using an AI-predicted IA value as a recommendation—not an automatic final decision.
* Applying an approved IA to the correct trade source system.
* Monitoring whether each downstream update succeeds.

AI should prepare the analysis and suggested value, but the appropriate user must authorize the decision.

## Risk

Risk users concentrate on:

* High-value margin calls.
* Specific watched counterparties or accounts.
* Material day-on-day movements.
* Understanding what is driving a call.
* Identifying whether the movement is attributable to a product, trade, position or trader.
* Reviewing MORS commentary.
* Requesting additional evidence or explanation.
* Escalating concerns.
* Confirming that risk oversight requirements have been satisfied.

Risk should not begin with a generic dashboard or report. Risk should begin with the accounts and calls requiring judgment.

## Slide 2 — Our most complex software is already moving from commands to intent

Use the developer experience as the opening analogy.

Visual idea:

* On one side, suggest the complexity of Visual Studio, IntelliJ or VS Code: menus, commands, panels, extensions and shortcuts.
* On the other side, show a simple intent such as:

> “Find the cause, make the change, run the tests and show me the evidence.”

The claim is not that developers have stopped using IDEs.

The defensible message is:

> The workbench remains, but the centre of interaction is shifting from locating commands to expressing intent.

Speaker narrative:

“Some of the most complex applications ever created are developer IDEs. They accumulated thousands of commands, panels and configuration options. Increasingly, however, the developer begins with an intention. The system explores the context, proposes changes, runs checks and returns the evidence. The complexity remains. What changes is the user’s relationship with it.”

## Slide 3 — The complexity did not disappear. The user’s job changed.

Contrast the old user responsibility with the future responsibility.

Old model:

* Find the correct system.
* Navigate to the right function.
* Assemble the data.
* Apply filters.
* Reconstruct the context.
* Interpret the result.
* Find the next action.
* Execute it manually.

Future model:

* Review what requires judgment.
* Inspect evidence.
* Challenge or refine the recommendation.
* Authorize or reject the action.
* Monitor the outcome.

Prominent line:

> **The user moves from assembling the work to exercising judgment over prepared work.**

## Slide 4 — The best AI is embedded in the journey

Use Google Maps or navigation as a brief outside-industry analogy.

The user states a destination. The application:

* Understands intent.
* Combines live signals.
* Recommends a route.
* Surfaces exceptions.
* Recalculates when conditions change.
* Allows the user to override.

The user is not required to inspect every possible road.

Connect the analogy to enterprise work:

> A margin analyst should not have to inspect every call to discover the eight that matter.

Avoid spending too much time on the analogy. Its purpose is to make embedded, proactive intelligence feel familiar.

## Slide 10 — Conversation should belong to the account

Introduce the Account Room concept.

Today:

* Evidence is in the application.
* Conversation is in Teams.
* Instructions are in email.
* Decisions may be retained in individual memory.
* The audit trail must be reconstructed later.

Future:

* MORS, Risk and Collateral Operations participate in one account- or call-specific conversation.
* The AI assistant participates using the same account context.
* AI answers only when grounded evidence is available.
* AI cites the supporting records.
* Decisions, approvals and execution outcomes remain attached to the account.
* Teams can be integrated as another participation surface.

“Let me begin with one of the most complex categories of software many of us use: the developer IDE—Visual Studio, IntelliJ or VS Code.

These applications contain thousands of commands, panels, shortcuts and configuration options. But increasingly, I begin my work with a sentence:

‘Find the cause, make the change, run the tests and show me the evidence.’

Why can’t the system say:

That is the future-state concept I want to explore.”
